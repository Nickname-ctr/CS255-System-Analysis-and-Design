# DriverPass: Business Requirements and System Design

This repository contains my work for the DriverPass project in CS 255: a Business Requirements Document (BRD) and a System Design Document (SDD). Below are my reflections on the project.

## Briefly summarize the DriverPass project. Who was the client? What type of system did they want you to design?

The client was DriverPass, a new driver-training company represented by its owner, Liam, and its IT officer, Ian. DriverPass was started because many people fail their DMV driving test (the company's research suggests more than 65% of applicants do), and no single service combines classroom-style instruction, practice testing, and real on-the-road training. They wanted a cloud-hosted web system where customers can register, choose one of three training packages, take online classes and practice tests, and book two-hour in-car lessons with an assigned driver and car from the company's fleet of ten vehicles. The system also needed to give staff (owner, IT officer, secretary, and drivers) the tools to manage reservations, customer accounts, driver notes, and activity reports, and to receive updates from the DMV so the training content stays current. My job was to turn that interview into a BRD, then into an SDD with UML diagrams (use case, two activity diagrams, sequence, and class) and technical requirements covering hardware, software, tools, and infrastructure.

## What did you do particularly well?

I think I did best at making the documents consistent with each other and traceable back to what the client said. Each requirement in the BRD, such as preventing double-booking, logging who created, canceled, or changed a reservation, and locking an account after repeated failed logins, shows up again in the SDD in a diagram or in the "How the Design Meets the Requirements" table, so a developer can see why each design decision exists. I also tried to be careful about separating what the client actually said from what I was proposing. Targets like the 3-second page response time, 99% availability, and the five-attempt lockout are labeled as proposed and pending DriverPass's approval rather than being presented as facts. The Assumptions and Limitations sections follow the same idea: they call out things like the unconfirmed DMV update format and the fact that drivers were mentioned in the interview but not listed as a formal user role. Finally, the activity diagram for password reset is a small detail I'm happy with, since it shows the system giving the same message whether or not an account exists, which protects user IDs from being guessed.

## If you could choose one part of your work on these documents to revise, what would you pick? How would you improve it?

I would revise how I handled the open business questions, especially cancellation and refund policy and how cancellations affect a customer's remaining package hours. The design checks `hoursRemaining` when a reservation is booked, but the SDD only notes that how cancellations affect those hours is a DriverPass policy still to be defined. That leaves a gap in the reservation flow that the sequence and activity diagrams do not fully cover. To improve it, I would go back to Liam with a short list of specific policy questions (cancellation windows, refunds, no-shows, and lesson availability rules), then update the use case, activity diagram, and class diagram so that canceling a reservation has a clearly defined effect on hours and billing. I would also number each requirement so the table in the SDD could reference exact requirement IDs instead of summaries, which would make the traceability even stronger.

## How did you interpret the user's needs and implement them into your system design? Why is it so important to consider the user's needs when designing?

I started by identifying who the users were and what each one needs to do: customers register, pick a package, book lessons, and track test progress; the secretary does the same on a customer's behalf when they call; drivers view schedules and enter lesson comments; the owner needs activity reports and the ability to disable a package; and the IT officer needs full control to reset passwords and block accounts, such as for an employee who has left. Those needs became role-based access in the class diagram (a User superclass with a subclass for each role), a use case diagram showing each actor's tasks, and a user interface based on Liam's own sketch, with test progress, customer information, driver notes, and special needs and photos in the layout he described. Some needs were stated indirectly, so I translated them into technical choices. For example, "view data from anywhere but only change it online, to avoid redundant data on different servers" became a browser-based cloud application with downloadable reports for offline use and changes disabled when there is no connection. Considering the user's needs matters because a system can be technically correct and still fail if it does not fit how people actually work. Features no one asked for add cost and complexity, and missing features lead to workarounds, errors, and a system nobody wants to use. In DriverPass's case, the whole business depends on customers finding scheduling easy and staff trusting the data.

## How do you approach designing software? What techniques or strategies would you use in the future to analyze and design a system?

I like to work from the outside in: start with the client's problem and goals, identify the users and their tasks, write requirements, and only then choose diagrams and technology. In this project that meant moving from the interview, to the BRD (functional and nonfunctional requirements, assumptions, limitations, and a Gantt chart), to UML models, to technical requirements. Each UML diagram answered a different question: the use case diagram shows who does what, the activity diagrams show the flow and decision points, the sequence diagram shows how the components interact over time (including a conflict re-check at save time so two people cannot book the same slot), and the class diagram shows the data and responsibilities behind it. In the future I would keep using that layered approach, and I would add a few more practices: confirming requirements with the client early and often, including walking them through the diagrams and a prototype or wireframe before building; writing acceptance criteria for every requirement so it can be tested; ranking requirements by priority so scope decisions are easier when time is short (the DriverPass schedule had only 11 days for testing); and thinking through security and failure cases early rather than adding them at the end. Working in small, reviewable iterations, rather than trying to get every document perfect in one pass, would also help catch gaps like the cancellation-policy question above sooner.
